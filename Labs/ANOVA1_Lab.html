Overview

You’ll be working with data adapted from studies on emotion recognition (see here). Specifically, you’re testing 2 research questions:

RQ1: Does the region of the face covered affect a child’s ability to recognize emotions conveyed by facial expressions?

RQ2: Does the type of emotion being expressed affect a child’s ability to recognize it?

To investigate this, you recruited a sample of primary-school aged children. All participants were presented with photos of people making facial expressions associated with 5 different emotions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, disgust).

However, participants were grouped into one of three conditions. Participants in the first condition viewed unobstructed faces. Participants in the second condition viewed faces wearing sunglasses so that the eyes were obstructed. Participants in the third condition viewed faces wearing masks so that the mouth was obstructed (see examples below).

Photos were presented in random order for each participant.

Conduct Between-Subjects ANOVA

As you are comparing three independent means, you’ll use results from a between-subjects One-Way ANOVA to provide statistical support for your Research Question 1 hypothesis. Recall that you’re testing the following hypothesis:

\(H_1\) - At least one of the following is true:

  • \(\mu_{Control} \neq \mu_{EyesCovered}\)
  • \(\mu_{Control} \neq \mu_{MouthCovered}\)
  • \(\mu_{MouthCovered} \neq \mu_{EyesCovered}\)

The requirements/assumptions of a traditional (Fisher’s) One-Way ANOVA are:

  1. Normality: Residuals are normally distributed

  2. Independence of Observations: Individual observations should not be dependent upon any others

  3. Homogeneity of Variance: Variances within each group should be generally similar

  4. The dependent/outcome variable must be continuous and the independent/predictor variable must be categorical

Independence

Assessing independence requires you to have an understanding of the conditions under which your data were collected. Good study design increases the likelihood that the observations are independent of each other. For the purposes of this example, you can consider this assumption to be met.


Homogeneity of Variance

Fisher’s ANOVA requires homogeneity of variance between groups. When group sizes are equal, this test is highly robust to heterogeneity of variance. However, with unequal group sizes, violations of this assumption can be problematic and the test may produce inaccurate results if this assumption is violated.

There are a few options of assessing/dealing with homogeneity of variance in this case.

  1. Welch’s ANOVA is robust to violations of homogeneity. Rather than checking this assumption, Welch’s ANOVA can be used by default. This can be set up by running the test using the Analyses>ANOVA>One-Way ANOVA. However, you cannot currently produce effect sizes for a Welch’s ANOVA in jamovi, so you would need to rely on an external effect size calculator, such as this one.

  2. Alternatively, you can use Levene’s test to check for homogeneity in the Analyses>ANOVA>ANOVA tab. To do this, select ‘Homogeneity test’ under the ‘Assumptions Checks’ section. If the test is not significant, you can assume your data meet the requirement of homogeneity. Note that this test is subject to sample size, and could produce biased results when samples are especially large or small. However, it is a commonly used method of assessing heterogeneity and can be used for the purposes of this course. It should be used alongside visualisations of residuals plots, which can be produced under Analyses>Regression>Linear Regression (see the Fitted vs Residuals plot).

Conduct Repeated-Measures ANOVA

To address Research Question 2, you will test for differences between 5 paired means. To do this, you’ll use from a repeated-measures One-Way ANOVA. Recall that you’re testing the following hypothesis:

There is an effect of emotion type (levels: Happiness, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Disgust) on a child’s ability to recognise emotions in masked individuals.

\(H_1:\) at least one \(\mu \neq\) the other \(\mu s\)

The requirements/assumptions of a Repeated-Measures One-Way ANOVA are:

  1. Normality: Residuals are normally distributed

  2. Sphericity: Variances within each group should be generally similar

  3. The dependent/outcome variable must be continuous and the independent/predictor variable must be categorical

  4. Observations must be matched/paired within individuals

Independence

Independence is not assumed between responses, since all participants are providing multiple data points. However, independence is still assumed between participants. As with a between-subjects ANOVA, this is handled through good study design.


Sphericity

Unfortunately, Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity has the same disadvantages of Levene’s test and should be interpreted in the context of sample size, visualisations, and standard deviation values when possible. However, it is a commonly used method to check this assumption. To check for sphericity in the data, tick the ‘Sphericity tests’ option under ‘Assumptions Checks’.

As with Levene’s test, the null hypothesis is that the assumption of sphericity is not violated, so a non-significant result (\(p\) > .05) suggests we can consider this assumption to be met.

Interpret & Report

Now that you’ve completed the analyses, write a mini results section describing your findings. In this section, please:

  • Give a brief description of your sample as you would in a methods section.

  • Report your results and a provide interpretation in APA style.

Click here for a hint

There are many ways you could write this up, but you’ll need to at least include the following information:

  • alpha level
  • power analysis information (including effect size, sample size, power, and alpha)
  • number of participants (n)
  • participant demographics (mean age, age sd, and age range of sample; gender distribution of sample)
  • results from assumptions checks
  • descriptive data for each IV group (M and SD)
  • the type of test used
  • independent and dependent variables tested
  • \(F\)-statistic
  • degrees of freedom
  • p-values
  • effect sizes
  • posthoc results

The proper style of reporting the results from an ANOVA looks like this:

\(F\)([between groups \(df\)], [within groups \(df\)]) = [\(F\)-statistic], \(p\) = [\(p\)-value], \(\eta^2\) = [\(\eta^2\) value].

Note that the numbers inside the curly brackets refer to the degrees of freedom. ANOVA has two types of \(df\) that should be reported. Please refer to the lecture slides for a specific example.
